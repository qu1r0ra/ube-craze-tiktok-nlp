Purple used to mean something specific in the Philippines. It meant your lola stirring a pot for hours until her arm ached. It meant halo-halo on a humid afternoon, the ice melting faster than you could eat it. It meant a color you tasted before you could name it.

***What is Ube? : (SHIN)***    
Before it became a global food trend, before it appeared in cafés, bakeries, and social media feeds around the world, ube was already a cherished part of Filipino life. Ube, scientifically known as Dioscorea alata, is a species of purple yam that has been cultivated in the Philippines for centuries. While it is grown in other tropical regions, it has become one of the most recognizable ingredients in Filipino cuisine because of its rich flavor, vibrant violet color, and deep cultural significance. Unlike purple sweet potatoes, which are often mistaken for ube, true ube has a naturally creamy texture and a distinct sweet, nutty, and slightly vanilla-like flavor. 

These qualities make it a favorite ingredient in many traditional Filipino desserts. For generations, Filipinos have transformed ube into beloved delicacies such as ube halaya, halo-halo, sapin-sapin, hopia, ice cream, cakes, breads, and pastries. Preparing these dishes is often a family tradition, especially during holidays, fiestas, birthdays, and other celebrations. But ube is more than just a dessert ingredient. 

For many Filipinos, it represents childhood memories, family gatherings, and moments spent around the dining table. The aroma of freshly cooked ube halaya may remind someone of helping their grandparents in the kitchen. A bowl of halo-halo might bring back memories of hot summer afternoons shared with family and friends. These experiences give ube a meaning that goes far beyond its taste or appearance. Food has always been one of the strongest expressions of culture. Every traditional recipe carries stories, values, and memories that are passed from one generation to the next. Through ube, Filipinos preserve not only culinary traditions but also a sense of identity and belonging. To the rest of the world, ube may simply be another colorful dessert. But to many Filipinos, it feels like home.

***History of Ube: (CLAIRE)***  
While ube is now known around the world for its distinct color and falvor, its story in the Philippines goes back much further.

Ube, scientifically known as Dioscorea alta, is a purple yam that has been cultivated and consumed in the Philippines for centuries. Its history goes beyond its popularity in modern Filipino desserts, as it has long been part of Philippine agriculture, food traditions, and local communities.

Historical records show that ube was already known and eaten by Filipinos during the early Spanish colonial period. It was even included in the first Tagalog-Spanish dictionary published in 1613, suggesting that it was already a familiar part of local food culture. Over time, Filipinos found different ways to prepare ube and included it in various traditional dishes and desserts.

Ube also became an important food source for communities during difficult times. In bohol. The kinampay variety became closely associated with the local culture. It was especially valued because it could provide food during droughts when other crops could not survive. Its importance to the community continues today through traditions such as the Ubi Festival.

As Filipino cuisine continued to develop, ube became a common ingredient in foods such as ube halaya, halo-halo, cakes, and ice cream. Today, ube has gained popularity around the world and is widely associated with Filipino cuisine. However, its popularity today is rooted in a much longer history of farming, food traditions, and community practices in the Philippines.

Because of this, ube is more than just a popular food ingredient. Its history reflects the connection between Filipino agriculture, culture, and food identity, as well as the farmers and communities who have continued to grow and preserve it through generations.

***How Ube Went Global: (VENICE)***  
So how did a root vegetable grown in Philippine soil end up on menus across four continents? The honest answer is: people carried it there.

For decades, Filipino families have settled far from home — in the United States, the Middle East, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia. And wherever they went, they brought their recipes with them, because food travels the way memory does: quietly, in suitcases, in handwritten notes, in the muscle memory of hands that already know the measurements by heart. Long before ube was a trend, it was already surviving, one household at a time, inside Filipino communities abroad.

What changed the scale of things was what happened next: those home recipes met open kitchens. Take Third Culture Bakery — it started nine years ago as a two-person operation in Berkeley, California, run by an immigrant couple who folded ube into their mochi donuts alongside matcha and black sesame. Today it's one of the Bay Area's most talked-about bakeries, with lines out the door and a loyal following built almost entirely on people filming what they bought. That's the pattern that repeats itself across the diaspora: someone's family recipe becomes a small business, and a small business becomes a bridge.

Eventually, that bridge reaches further than anyone filming a mochi donut could have expected. In early 2023, Magnolia Bakery — a chain with locations from Manhattan to Dubai — added an ube cheesecake to its menu. It wasn't a Filipino company introducing the ingredient anymore; it was one of the industry's most recognizable names deciding ube was worth putting its own label on. That's usually the sign a trend has crossed from niche to mainstream — when a brand that doesn't need to take risks decides this one is safe to take.

And the numbers back up what the menus suggest. According to the Philippines' Department of Trade and Industry, ube product exports rose roughly 20 percent year over year, with nearly half of that volume — worth about $1.5 million — heading to the United States alone, double what it had been the year before. As one FAO representative put it while speaking in Baguio, you can now find "ube ice cream in New York, ube cakes in London, ube lattes in Tokyo." Three cities, three continents, one Filipino tuber.

Underneath all of it — the export charts, the bakery lines, the cheesecake on a five-star hotel dessert menu — is a much simpler throughline. Ube's rise wasn't engineered by a marketing team. It was carried by migration, shaped by diaspora hands, and only later picked up by an industry that noticed people were already paying attention. The trend the world sees today is really the visible tip of something much older — a food that has been quietly connecting Filipinos to home for generations, long before anyone was filming it.

***Social Media and Digital Gastronationalism: (CLAIRE)***  
Social media has changed the way people discover, share, and appreciate food. Digital platforms like Tiktok have transformed food into a visual phenomenon, launching regional ingredients like Filipino ube into global culinary trends. While this viral popularity offers international exposure and economic opportunities for local creators and businesses, it also connects directly to grastronationalism, the concept of using food as a symbol of cultural identity, heritage, and national pride.

When an ingredient goes coral purely for its aesthetic appeal, such as ube’s vibrant purple hue, or labeling it as an exotic ingredient, it risks being commodified as a trendy novelty. This visual obsession can lead to cultural appropriation, stripping the food of its history and obscuring its Filipino origins. However, social media simultaneously equips native communities with a platform to counter this erasure. By sharing authentic recipes, stories, and educational content, Filipinos can actively reclaim ube’s narrative and ensure its heritage is respected globally.

Similar dynamics appear in other international food debates, such as the online disputes between South Korea and China regarding the origins and naming of kimchi. This controversy demonstrates how a traditional dish can quickly become a battleground for national identity when its cultural roots are challenged. Ultimately, digital gastronationalism extends far beyond simply marketing regional cuisine. As traditional foods become global trends, digital platforms provide communities with the essential tools needed to defend their heritage, educate international audiences, and safeguard their cultural identity.
